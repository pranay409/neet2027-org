Molecular Basis of Inheritance for NEET

DNA, RNA and Gene Expression Explained

Subject: Biology | Published: October 02, 2026

The molecular basis of inheritance is one of the most critical topics for NEET aspirants, carrying approximately 8-12% weightage in the exam. Understanding DNA structure, replication, RNA synthesis, and gene expression mechanisms is essential not only for scoring marks but also for comprehending the foundational principles of biology. This comprehensive guide breaks down these complex concepts into exam-focused explanations.

DNA Structure and Organization NCERT Ch. 6

DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) is the hereditary material carrying genetic information for all living organisms. NEET consistently tests the structural features of DNA that candidates must memorize precisely.

Double Helix Model and Chargaff's Rules

Watson and Crick proposed the double helix structure of DNA in 1953. DNA consists of two antiparallel polynucleotide strands wound around each other in a helical pattern. Each nucleotide contains a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogenous base. The bases pair specifically: Adenine (A) with Thymine (T) through 2 hydrogen bonds, and Guanine (G) with Cytosine (C) through 3 hydrogen bonds.

Chargaff's rules state that in DNA: amount of A = amount of T, and amount of G = amount of C. This 1:1 ratio is crucial for understanding DNA replication and is a frequent NEET question format where you calculate base composition given one base percentage.

Chromosome Structure and DNA Packaging

DNA in eukaryotic cells is packaged with histone proteins to form chromatin. The basic repeating unit is the nucleosome, consisting of 147 base pairs of DNA wrapped around a histone octamer (2 copies each of H2A, H2B, H3, and H4). Histone H1 acts as a linker connecting nucleosomes. The 30nm chromatin fiber further condenses into higher-order structures, ultimately forming visible chromosomes during cell division. This hierarchical packaging reduces 2 meters of DNA to fit within the nucleus.

⭐ NEET EXAM TIP

DNA packaging question pattern: Questions often ask the number of nucleosomes formed or how many bp are covered. Remember: 1 nucleosome = 147 bp (wrapped) + 54 bp (linker DNA) = 200 bp total average.

Common mistake: Confusing the number of histone proteins per nucleosome (8 total: 2 of each type) versus the total histone types (5: H1, H2A, H2B, H3, H4).

DNA Replication and Gene Regulation NCERT Ch. 6

Semi-conservative DNA replication ensures accurate transmission of genetic material. Meselson and Stahl's 1958 experiment using nitrogen isotopes definitively proved the semi-conservative mechanism, a topic frequently appearing in NEET's section C (assertion-reasoning questions).

Mechanism of DNA Replication

Replication begins at the origin of replication (ori) where DNA unwinds. DNA helicase breaks hydrogen bonds between bases, creating a replication fork. Single-stranded binding proteins prevent re-annealing. DNA polymerase III synthesizes the leading strand continuously (3' to 5' direction) and the lagging strand discontinuously as Okazaki fragments (1000-2000 bp in prokaryotes, 100-200 bp in eukaryotes). DNA polymerase I removes RNA primers and fills gaps. DNA ligase seals nicks between fragments.

Key enzymes and their functions are heavily tested: Helicase (unwinding), Primase (RNA primer synthesis), DNA Polymerase III (main synthesis in prokaryotes), Topoisomerase (relieving tension), and Ligase (joining). NEET questions often ask what happens when specific enzymes are inhibited.

Telomeres and Telomerase

Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences (TTAGGG in humans) at chromosome ends protecting genetic material. The "end-replication problem" occurs because DNA polymerase cannot fully replicate the 5' end of the lagging strand, leading to gradual telomere shortening. Telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein, adds telomeric repeats in germ cells and some somatic cells. This explains why cells can divide only a limited number of times (Hayflick limit), relevant to understanding cellular aging and cancer.

Transcription, Translation, and Gene Expression NCERT Ch. 6-7

The central dogma of molecular biology (DNA → RNA → Protein) underpins all gene expression. NEET dedicates substantial marks to understanding each step with regulatory mechanisms.

Transcription: From DNA to RNA

Transcription synthesizes RNA from a DNA template using RNA polymerase. In prokaryotes, a single RNA polymerase handles all transcription; in eukaryotes, three polymerases exist: RNA Pol I (rRNA genes), RNA Pol II (mRNA and most non-coding RNAs), and RNA Pol III (tRNA and 5S rRNA). Transcription proceeds in three stages: initiation (RNA polymerase binds to promoter), elongation (nucleotide addition), and termination (at specific sequences like AATAAA in eukaryotes).

Eukaryotic mRNA undergoes processing: 5' capping (7-methylguanosine addition), 3' polyadenylation (200-250 adenine nucleotides added), and splicing (removal of introns, joining of exons by snRNPs). This explains why pre-mRNA is much longer than mature mRNA and why mutations in splice sites cause genetic diseases—a concept NEET loves testing.

The Three Types of RNA

mRNA (messenger RNA): Carries genetic code from DNA to ribosomes. Codons (3-nucleotide sequences) specify amino acids. NEET often tests codon-anticodon pairing and the degeneracy of the genetic code (multiple codons for same amino acid). There are 64 codons: 61 sense codons, 3 stop codons (UAA, UAG, UGA), and 1 start codon (AUG).

tRNA (transfer RNA): Brings amino acids to ribosomes. Each tRNA has an anticodon complementary to mRNA codons and an amino acid attachment site (acceptor stem). The wobble base pairing at the third codon position allows one tRNA to recognize multiple codons. This is why fewer than 61 tRNAs exist (typically 40-50).

rRNA (ribosomal RNA): Component of ribosomes with catalytic functions. Combined with ribosomal proteins, rRNA forms the 70S ribosome (prokaryotes) or 80S ribosome (eukaryotes).

Translation: Protein Synthesis

Translation occurs at ribosomes in three phases: initiation (ribosome binds mRNA at start codon), elongation (tRNA brings amino acids, peptide bonds form), and termination (release factors recognize stop codons). The ribosome has three tRNA binding sites: A (amino acyl, incoming tRNA), P (peptidyl, tRNA with growing chain), and E (exit, departing tRNA).

Initiation requires initiation factors (IF2 in prokaryotes), the ribosome, and formyl-methionine-tRNA (fMet-tRNA) in prokaryotes or methionine-tRNA in eukaryotes. Elongation involves aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases (charging tRNAs with correct amino acids) and elongation factors (EF-Tu and EF-G in prokaryotes). Termination occurs when a stop codon enters the A site, recognized by release factors.

⭐ EXAM-CRITICAL CONCEPT

Genetic Code Properties (always tested):

  • Triplet: 3 nucleotides code for 1 amino acid
  • Degenerate: Multiple codons code for same amino acid (except Met and Trp)
  • Universal: Nearly same in all organisms (with rare exceptions)
  • Non-overlapping: Each nucleotide part of only one codon
  • Comma-less: No nucleotides between codons

Practice NEET's mutation questions: identify if mutations are silent, missense, or nonsense based on codon changes.

Gene Expression Regulation and Mutations NCERT Ch. 7

Gene expression regulation determines which genes are active and at what levels. NEET tests regulatory mechanisms from molecular to phenotypic levels with increasing difficulty in numerical problems.

Prokaryotic Gene Regulation: The Operon Model

The lac operon elegantly illustrates gene regulation. In E. coli, three structural genes (lacZ, lacY, lacA) produce enzymes for lactose metabolism. A repressor protein (encoded by lacI) binds to the operator region when lactose is absent, blocking transcription. When lactose (or allolactose) is present, it acts as an inducer, binding the repressor and causing it to release from the operator, allowing transcription. Glucose presence simultaneously causes catabolite repression through reduced cAMP levels, preventing full activation even with lactose present.

This regulatory pattern represents negative inducible control. The trp operon demonstrates negative repressible control: tryptophan (corepressor) binds the trp repressor, enabling operator binding and transcription inhibition. NEET frequently presents scenarios requiring students to predict expression patterns given different nutrient conditions.

Eukaryotic Gene Regulation

Eukaryotic regulation is more complex, involving transcription factors, enhancers, silencers, and chromatin remodeling. Enhancers are DNA sequences that increase transcription regardless of orientation or distance. Chromatin structure itself regulates access: heterochromatin is transcriptionally inactive while euchromatin is active.
