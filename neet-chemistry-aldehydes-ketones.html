Aldehydes, Ketones and Carboxylic Acids for NEET

Reaction Mechanisms Simplified

📅 October 25, 2026 📚 NCERT Class 12 ⏱️ 8 min read

Aldehydes, ketones, and carboxylic acids form the backbone of organic chemistry questions in NEET. These functional groups appear in nearly 15-20% of the chemistry paper, with emphasis on reaction mechanisms, preparation methods, and distinguishing tests. Understanding the underlying mechanisms rather than memorizing reactions is crucial for scoring consistently in this chapter.

The carbonyl group (C=O) is the defining feature that connects all three functional groups, but their reactivity patterns differ significantly due to structural differences. This article breaks down the essential reaction mechanisms that NEET examiners focus on, helping you tackle mechanism-based questions with confidence.

Nucleophilic Addition Reactions in Aldehydes and Ketones

NCERT Reference: Class 12 Chemistry, Chapter 12 - Aldehydes, Ketones and Carboxylic Acids (Sections 12.2-12.4)

The carbonyl group in aldehydes and ketones is highly polarized due to the electronegativity difference between carbon and oxygen. This makes the carbonyl carbon susceptible to nucleophilic attack—a cornerstone mechanism in NEET questions.

Why Aldehydes are More Reactive Than Ketones

Aldehydes contain one alkyl group and one hydrogen attached to the carbonyl carbon, while ketones have two alkyl groups. This structural difference has profound effects:

  • Steric hindrance: Ketones have bulkier groups around the carbonyl carbon, slowing nucleophilic approach
  • Electronic effects: Alkyl groups donate electron density through inductive effects, making the carbonyl carbon less electrophilic in ketones
  • Hydration tendency: Aldehydes form stable hydrates; ketones rarely do (unless strongly activated)

NEET questions frequently test this reactivity difference through comparative reactions. When asked which compound undergoes nucleophilic addition faster or which forms a stable product, aldehydes are typically the answer.

Mechanism of Nucleophilic Addition

The three-step mechanism occurs as follows:

  1. Nucleophilic attack: The nucleophile attacks the electrophilic carbonyl carbon from above or below the C=O plane (Bürgi-Dunitz angle of ~107°)
  2. Formation of tetrahedral intermediate: A negatively charged intermediate forms with sp³ hybridization at the former carbonyl carbon
  3. Protonation: The oxyanion is protonated by acidic conditions (or water), forming the final product

Common nucleophiles tested in NEET include CN⁻ (cyanide), H⁻ (hydride), NH₂OH, and alcohols. The mechanism remains the same; only the final product structure changes.

🎯 NEET Tip: When a mechanism question shows a tetrahedral intermediate with a negatively charged oxygen, you're looking at nucleophilic addition. For aldehyde/ketone questions, this intermediate is always the key intermediate to recognize. Drawing or identifying this intermediate correctly can fetch you full marks in mechanism-based questions.

Oxidation Reactions: The Critical Distinction

NCERT Reference: Class 12 Chemistry, Chapter 12 (Sections 12.5-12.6) and Chapter 8 (Redox Reactions)

Oxidation distinguishes aldehydes from ketones—a concept central to NEET differentiation questions. Aldehydes possess an oxidizable hydrogen attached to the carbonyl carbon; ketones do not.

Aldehyde Oxidation Pathways

Aldehydes are readily oxidized to carboxylic acids under mild conditions using:

  • KMnO₄ (potassium permanganate) in acidic or neutral conditions
  • K₂Cr₂O₇ (potassium dichromate) in acidic conditions
  • Tollens' reagent [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ (produces silver mirror—positive test)
  • Fehling's reagent (Cu²⁺ complex, reduces to Cu₂O brick-red precipitate)

The oxidation mechanism involves hydride transfer from the aldehyde to the oxidizing agent. In Tollens' test, the aldehyde is oxidized while Ag⁺ is reduced to Ag (silver mirror). This is a crucial distinguishing test that NEET frequently asks about.

Why Ketones Don't Undergo This Oxidation

Ketones lack a hydrogen on the carbonyl carbon, so they cannot be oxidized to carboxylic acids under mild conditions. Only under very stringent conditions (like hot concentrated KMnO₄) do ketones undergo C-C cleavage, producing smaller carboxylic acids. This difference is repeatedly tested through comparative questions.

Carboxylic Acid Reactions and Mechanism Insight

NCERT Reference: Class 12 Chemistry, Chapter 12 (Sections 12.7-12.10)

Carboxylic acids display dual reactivity: the carboxyl group (-COOH) functions both as a weak acid and undergoes nucleophilic acyl substitution reactions.

Esterification: Nucleophilic Acyl Substitution Mechanism

Esterification is the most tested mechanism involving carboxylic acids in NEET:

  1. Protonation of carbonyl oxygen: In acidic conditions, the C=O oxygen is protonated, increasing electrophilicity of the carbonyl carbon
  2. Nucleophilic attack: The alcohol oxygen attacks the carbonyl carbon, forming a tetrahedral intermediate
  3. Proton transfer: Internal rearrangement occurs within the intermediate
  4. Elimination: Water is eliminated from the intermediate
  5. Deprotonation: The ester product is formed after deprotonation

This Fischer esterification is reversible and reaches equilibrium. To drive the reaction forward, excess alcohol or removal of water is employed—concepts that NEET tests through mechanism and application questions.

Decarboxylation of Carboxylic Acids

Carboxylic acids lose CO₂ under specific conditions. NEET questions distinguish between:

  • Thermal decarboxylation: When heated with soda lime (NaOH + CaO), producing hydrocarbons
  • Beta-dicarbonyl decarboxylation: Compounds with electron-withdrawing groups ortho to the carboxyl group decarboxylate readily
  • Malonic ester synthesis decarboxylation: Used in organic synthesis to prepare carboxylic acids

The mechanism involves formation of a carbanion or stabilized intermediate that facilitates CO₂ departure. Understanding why certain carboxylic acids decarboxylate more readily than others is critical.

Practical NEET Exam Patterns and High-Scoring Strategies

Exam Pattern: NEET typically allocates 3-4 questions (12-16 marks) to this chapter across mechanism, preparation, properties, and tests

Recent NEET papers show a clear trend toward mechanism-based questions rather than simple factual recall. Questions often present partial mechanisms and ask for missing steps or intermediates.

Common Question Formats

  • Identification of reagents: "Which reagent converts aldehyde X to carboxylic acid?" Tests your knowledge of oxidation reagents
  • Mechanism completion: Showing an incomplete mechanism and asking what forms at specific steps
  • Comparative reactivity: "Why does compound A react faster than compound B?" Requires understanding electronic and steric effects
  • Multi-step synthesis: Converting one functional group to another through 2-3 steps
  • Test differentiation: Distinguishing aldehydes, ketones, and carboxylic acids using chemical tests
